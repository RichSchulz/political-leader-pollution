\begin{abstract}
This project studies whether political leaders direct cleaner growth to their birth regions while shifting pollution-intensive activity elsewhere. We combine PLAD birth-region information, harmonized nighttime lights, ACAG surface NO$_2$, V-Dem democracy scores, and GADM ADM2 boundaries to build a global district-year panel for 2005--2019. Preliminary results replicate the classic regional favoritism result in the long 1992--2013 nightlights sample. New nested-window diagnostics show that this is not just a 1990s artifact: the birth-region coefficient remains positive through 2000--2013 and collapses only in 2005--2013, pointing to a later-window attenuation rather than a simple decade break. In the restricted 2005--2019 pollution sample, we do not find evidence of ``green favoritism'': pooled NO$_2$ estimates are weakly positive at lag 0, pollution intensity is also weakly positive, autocracy-only results point in the same direction rather than toward cleaner growth, and event-study coefficients turn positive only several years after ascension while still showing some pre-period movement. A parallel PM$_{2.5}$ extension points in a similar direction: longer windows (1998--2024 and 1998--2019) show positive and statistically significant pollution effects, while the later 2005--2024 window is positive but null. The main empirical issue for the second half of the project is therefore not data access but interpretation: the original favoritism result weakens sharply in the post-2005 period, which constrains how cleanly we can interpret the environmental extension.
\end{abstract}

\section*{1. Project Status and Research Question}
Our research question is whether leaders channel cleaner forms of economic development to their own birth regions while leaving dirtier activity to politically peripheral districts. The intended empirical signature is a positive treatment effect on nighttime lights combined with a zero or negative treatment effect on pollution. The midterm milestone focused on building a proposal-aligned 2005--2019 panel using ACAG NO$_2$ data, harmonized nightlights, and PLAD treatment coding at ADM2 level.

The main progress since the proposal is that the data pipeline is now working end-to-end. The key empirical issue is now substantive rather than technical: the classic birth-region favoritism effect in nightlights is present in the long historical sample and survives through 2000--2013, but it largely disappears in the post-2005 window that overlaps the pollution analysis.

\section*{2. Annotated Bibliography}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Hodler and Raschky (2014), ``Regional Favoritism.''} This is the foundational paper for the project. It shows that national leaders direct more economic activity, proxied by nightlights, to their birth regions; our design is a direct environmental extension of this logic.

    \item \textbf{Bora (2025), ``Birthplace Favoritism Revisited.''} Bora provides the most relevant modern replication benchmark for the lights specification, revisiting the original 1992--2013 DMSP-era sample and extending the analysis with more recent satellite evidence and crisis-period heterogeneity. This paper matters because it helps benchmark whether our replication choices are close to the current literature.

    \item \textbf{Bomprezzi et al. (2025), PLAD.} PLAD provides leader birth-region identifiers at subnational level and makes a global ADM2 treatment design feasible. It is the core political dataset used to define treated birth districts and leader spells.

    \item \textbf{Bomprezzi et al. (2024), ``Wedded to Prosperity?''} This paper broadens the favoritism literature by showing that politically connected personal ties also shape subnational resource allocation. It is useful because it supports the broader idea that informal favoritism can operate through geography and personal networks.

    \item \textbf{Cooper et al. (2022), global fine-scale ambient NO$_2$.} This paper underlies the ACAG NO$_2$ product we now use. It is important because it delivers annual fine-resolution surface NO$_2$ estimates that are much better suited to an ADM2 panel than coarser satellite-only products.

    \item \textbf{Elvidge et al. (2013), ``Why VIIRS data are superior to DMSP.''} This paper is the key reference for the nightlights side of the project. It explains the DMSP--VIIRS sensor transition and why harmonization matters for any panel that spans both systems.

    \item \textbf{Copeland and Taylor (2004), ``Trade, Growth, and the Environment.''} This is not a favoritism paper, but it provides the environmental-economics intuition behind our project. We adapt the pollution-haven logic from cross-border settings to within-country political geography.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{4.1 First-Stage Replication Diagnostics}
The central empirical comparison concerns the original nightlights first stage and the later restricted sample used for the pollution analysis. In the long replication-style DMSP window, the estimated birth-region effect is positive and statistically significant. The nested-window diagnostics in Table~\ref{tab:first-stage} indicate that this pattern is not confined to the 1990s: the coefficient remains positive in 1995--2013 and persists, though more weakly, in 2000--2013. The sharp attenuation occurs only in 2005--2013.

\textbf{Interpretation of the nested-window diagnostics.} The replication holds in the long DMSP-era sample and does not disappear immediately once the 1990s are excluded. The birth-region coefficient remains positive in 1995--2013 and remains positive, though weaker, in 2000--2013. The sharp attenuation occurs only in 2005--2013. Accordingly, the evidence is more consistent with a later-period weakening of the first stage than with a result driven exclusively by the late 1990s.

\textbf{Implications and remaining explanations.} These diagnostics reduce the plausibility of two simple explanations. First, the attenuation is not driven by the DMSP--VIIRS transition, because 2005--2013 remains entirely within the DMSP era. Second, it is not well summarized by a sharp decade break: the pooled $BirthRegion \times post2000$ and $BirthRegion \times post2010$ interactions are both statistically insignificant in the full 1992--2013 sample. Remaining possibilities include a smaller number of treated observations and clusters in the later window, weaker favoritism in the later political period, changes in country or leader composition, and attenuation specific to the post-2005 period rather than to a single decade indicator.

\subsection*{4.2 Pollution-Side Results}
Because the first-stage lights effect collapses in the later window, the environmental interpretation is constrained. The pollution regressions should therefore be read as evidence about later-period pollution dynamics in leaders' birth regions, not as a clean test of ``green favoritism'' built on a strongly replicated favoritism first stage.

Table~\ref{tab:main-results} reports the main pooled estimates in the ACAG sample. These coefficients do not support ``green favoritism.'' The nightlights effect is essentially zero, while the contemporaneous NO$_2$ effect is weakly positive and statistically significant at the 5 percent level. The pollution-intensity outcome, defined as log NO$_2$ minus log nightlights, is also positive and marginally significant. In substantive terms, the current pooled estimates suggest either no special growth effect in birth regions or slightly dirtier contemporaneous growth, not cleaner development. The decade-interaction check from the replication notebook is therefore a supporting fact rather than a central result: it does not show a statistically distinct post-2000 or post-2010 break, and the nested-window comparison remains the clearest diagnostic.

The heterogeneity checks are useful because they test whether the missing first stage reappears where the theory would most strongly predict it. So far that does not happen. The democracy interaction shows no meaningful birth-region effect in either nightlights or NO$_2$, and the interaction term itself is close to zero. Restricting the sample to stricter autocracies produces somewhat larger pollution-side coefficients, but not cleaner ones: in the $v2x\_polyarchy < 0.3$ subsample, the pollution-intensity coefficient is positive and statistically significant. That pattern leans away from the original ``green favoritism'' hypothesis and more toward a weak dirty-growth interpretation in the narrowest autocracy sample.

The event studies point in the same direction, but they are not yet clean enough for a strong causal claim. The most notable positive NO$_2$ coefficients appear three to four years after leader ascension, and pollution intensity also turns positive around year four. However, there is already some movement in the pre-period, which means we cannot yet interpret the timing as clearly post-treatment. A separate PM$_{2.5}$ exercise reinforces the broader pattern rather than overturning it: in longer windows, the treatment effect on log PM$_{2.5}$ is positive and statistically significant (for example, 0.0078 with $p=0.011$ for 1998--2024 and 0.0091 with $p=0.002$ for 1998--2019), while the later 2005--2024 window yields positive but statistically insignificant coefficients. The narrowest defensible summary is therefore this: the data pipeline now works, the pollution outcomes are measurable, and across both NO$_2$ and PM$_{2.5}$ we do not find evidence that leaders channel cleaner growth to their birth regions.

\section*{5. Plan for Remaining Work}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Tighten the identification strategy by testing whether the baseline lights first stage reappears in narrower subsamples, especially strict autocracies and the DMSP-only 2005--2013 window.
    \item Expand the pollution-side robustness checks using alternative outcomes or transformations, including PM$_{2.5}$ as a possible alternative pollution proxy if the NO$_2$ framing remains weak.
    \item Build cleaner event-study figures with confidence intervals and a more explicit treatment-timing design.
    \item Decide on the final framing: either a narrower heterogeneity paper about autocratic favoritism and pollution, or a later-period null result showing attenuation of classic favoritism in the ACAG era.
    \item Convert the current notebook results into publication-style tables and a short empirical narrative for the final report.
\end{enumerate}
