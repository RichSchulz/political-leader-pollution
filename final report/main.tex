\begin{abstract}
We study how the canonical birth-region favoritism result in nightlights evolves over time. We combine the Political Leaders' Affiliation Database, ADM2-level nightlights, ACAG surface NO$_2$, ACAG PM$_{2.5}$, V-Dem democracy scores, and GADM administrative boundaries in a global district-year panel with region fixed effects and country-year fixed effects. In the long DMSP window, the lag-0 birth-region coefficient is positive at 0.0128 in 1992--2013 ($p = 0.030$). But 5-year interactions within DMSP show a sharper temporal pattern: the effect is strongest in 2007--2011 at 0.0292 ($p = 0.004$) and then falls to $-0.0057$ in 2012--2013 ($p = 0.709$), so the break begins before any sensor switch. The historical positive effect is concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa, where the 1992--2013 coefficient is 0.0442 ($p = 0.011$), while non-SSA is close to zero. Pollution outcomes do not support the idea that leaders shifted from directing more growth to directing cleaner growth toward birth regions: NO$_2$ and PM$_{2.5}$ estimates are zero to positive. The historical effect is further concentrated in countries in the top quartile of mineral rents: excluding them, the 1992--2013 coefficient falls to 0.0038 ($p = 0.526$), and the SSA effect collapses analogously, while SSA mineral-rent countries alone yield 0.0967 ($p < 0.001$). The combined evidence is consistent with a commodity-boom episode of fiscal-slack favoritism rather than a universal pattern.
\end{abstract}

Political leaders often direct resources toward their home regions, a pattern documented in cross-country evidence using subnational nightlights \cite{HodlerRaschky2014}. This paper asks whether that result still holds in later periods, and how it changes over time inside the original DMSP era. The post-2005 years are especially useful for that question because they overlap with harmonized nightlights and global pollution measures. They are also exactly where the canonical birth-region activity effect becomes much harder to detect.

The paper's central empirical result is not a smooth attenuation. Within DMSP, the birth-region effect is modest in early 5-year bins, strongest in 2007--2011, and then much weaker by 2012--2013. That break starts before the move to later nightlights products. The historical positive effect is also geographically concentrated: Sub-Saharan Africa drives the long-window replication, while non-SSA estimates are much smaller. In later annual windows, however, no targeted post-2005 subsample restores a precise positive activity effect.

Pollution outcomes enter as a diagnostic for one possible explanation. If leaders still favor birth regions after 2005, but do so through the composition rather than the quantity of growth, then birth regions may receive cleaner activity even when nightlights do not rise. We define ``green favoritism'' as lower pollution per unit of proxied economic activity in leader birth regions. This possibility matters for environmental inequality as well as distributive politics \cite{CopelandTaylor2004}. The pollution evidence does not support it: NO$_2$ and PM$_{2.5}$ estimates are zero to positive rather than negative.

Motivated by the late-2000s peak and the Sub-Saharan concentration, we ask whether the canonical effect is gated by exposure to mineral rents. The timing of the break lines up with the end of the 2000s commodity supercycle, and SSA is heavily over-represented among mineral-rent economies. This split serves as a diagnostic for a fiscal-slack interpretation of the temporal break: if the historical effect is concentrated in mineral-rich states, the late-2000s peak and the SSA concentration have a common cause. Excluding the top quartile of mineral-rent countries eliminates the long-window effect entirely, and the surviving variation lies almost exclusively in mineral-rich SSA economies.

The contribution relative to recent replication work is therefore diagnostic. Recent work with expanded birthplace data and harmonized nightlights also finds a positive pooled favoritism effect in extended samples \cite{Bora2025, DubenHodlerRaschky2026}. By contrast, we focus on the temporal pattern inside later annual windows, show that the break starts within DMSP before any sensor switch, isolate the long-run Sub-Saharan African historical core, and identify mineral rents as the political-economic configuration that generates the canonical effect. The result is not that the historical finding disappears in all data. It is that the later-period relationship is unstable: it peaks in the late 2000s, weakens sharply by the early 2010s, is concentrated in mineral-rent SSA economies, and is not restored by post-2005 subsample cuts or by standalone VIIRS data. The mineral-rent decomposition is the paper's main mechanism contribution.

\section{Data and Empirical Design}

The analysis combines five core inputs. First, the Political Leaders' Affiliation Database (PLAD) provides leader identity, spell timing, and subnational birthplace information used to code treatment \cite{Bomprezzi2025PLAD}. Second, nightlights provide the economic-activity proxy. We use a replication-style DMSP ADM2 panel for 1992--2013, a harmonized DMSP-to-VIIRS panel for 2005--2019, and a standalone VIIRS ADM2 panel for 2012--2024 as a single-sensor later diagnostic. DMSP records luminance as an integer Digital Number (DN) from 0 to 63, with known saturation near the ceiling \cite{Gibson2021}. Third, we use ACAG surface NO$_2$ data for the 2005--2019 pollution window \cite{Cooper2022NO2}. Fourth, we use ACAG SatPM PM$_{2.5}$ as a longer-window pollution extension. Fifth, GADM ADM2 boundaries provide the common subnational geography \cite{GADM2022}.

Treatment is defined at the district-year level. A district is treated when it is the birth region of the national leader in office in that year. To ensure a unique treatment assignment in transition years, we truncate the earlier of any two consecutive leader spells that overlap within a country.

The core estimating equation uses ADM2 fixed effects and country-year fixed effects:
\[
\log(Y_{i c t} + 0.01) = \beta \, BirthRegionLeader_{i c t} + \alpha_i + \gamma_{c t} + \varepsilon_{i c t},
\]
where $i$ indexes ADM2 regions, $c$ countries, and $t$ years. With ADM2 and country-year fixed effects, $\beta$ is identified by changes in the current leader's birth district relative to other districts in the same country-year, net of permanent district differences. A causal interpretation requires birth and non-birth districts to have followed parallel within-country trends absent leader ascension. That assumption could fail if future leaders come from districts already on different trajectories or if national transitions coincide with region-specific shocks, so we interpret the estimates as reduced-form evidence rather than a fully causal decomposition.

Standard errors are clustered by leader spell in the baseline, following the leader-term clustering convention in recent replication work such as \cite{Bora2025}. Because \cite{HodlerRaschky2014} and \cite{DubenHodlerRaschky2026} use country-level clustering, we also report country-clustered robustness checks for the main estimates. Following the baseline specification in \cite{HodlerRaschky2014}, we estimate lag 0, lag 1, and lag 2 treatment indicators, and focus on lag 0 in the main text as the most direct test of the regional favoritism hypothesis.

Our later-period environmental outcomes are log nightlights, log NO$_2$, and a pollution-intensity measure defined as $\log(\text{NO}_2 + 0.01) - \log(\text{Nightlights} + 0.01)$. Because both component regressions use the same regressor and fixed effects, the intensity coefficient is mechanically a linear combination of the NO$_2$ and nightlights coefficients ($\beta_{intensity} = \beta_{NO2} - \beta_{lights}$). We report this measure as a compact summary of whether pollution rises more or less than nightlights in birth regions. Because it is constructed directly from the pollution and nightlights outcomes, it is not an independent third test. In the PM$_{2.5}$ extension, the analogous outcome is $\log(\text{PM}_{2.5} + 0.01) - \log(\text{Nightlights} + 0.01)$.

\section{Main Results}

\subsection*{Within-DMSP rise and break}

The long DMSP replication remains intact, but its internal time pattern is sharper than a simple before-and-after attenuation story. Figure~\ref{fig:dmsp-bins} estimates the lag-0 birth-region coefficient separately for 5-year bins inside the DMSP-only 1992--2013 sample. The coefficient is modest in 1992--1996 (0.0085, $p = 0.472$), 1997--2001 (0.0109, $p = 0.225$), and 2002--2006 (0.0107, $p = 0.196$), then rises to 0.0292 in 2007--2011 ($p = 0.004$). In the final, shorter 2012--2013 bin, it falls to $-0.0057$ ($p = 0.709$). The difference between 2007--2011 and 2012--2013 is itself large and statistically significant ($-0.0349$, $p = 0.005$). The basic break therefore begins within DMSP, before any switch to later nightlights products.

Pooled nested windows are consistent with this bin pattern: long DMSP windows ending in 2013 are positive (0.0128 in 1992--2013, $p = 0.030$), while pooled windows ending after the break collapse toward zero (0.0017 in 2005--2013, $p = 0.768$; 0.0004 in harmonized 2005--2019, $p = 0.966$). The later pooled null therefore reflects the late-2000s peak and early-2010s break visible in the bins, not a gradual monotonic fade.

\subsection*{Sub-Saharan Africa versus the rest}

The historical positive effect is concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa. Table~\ref{tab:ssa-decomp} shows that in DMSP 1992--2013, the Sub-Saharan Africa coefficient is 0.0442 ($p = 0.011$), while the non-SSA coefficient is only 0.0053 ($p = 0.370$). This is the cleanest decomposition of the original long-window result: the classic positive effect is largely an SSA phenomenon.

That historical concentration does not translate into a clean post-2005 rescue. In DMSP 2005--2013, both SSA and non-SSA are close to zero. In harmonized 2005--2019, SSA turns positive again at 0.0315, but remains imprecise ($p = 0.135$). The longer SSA-only harmonized grid shows that long windows still recover the historical pattern: the SSA 1992--2019 coefficient is 0.0448 ($p = 0.010$). The problem is therefore not that the historical SSA signal disappears everywhere. It is that short post-2005 windows do not preserve it cleanly.

\subsection*{Mineral rents and the late-2000s peak}

Do the temporal break and the SSA concentration share a common driver? Sub-Saharan Africa is heavily over-represented among mineral-dependent economies, and the 2007--2011 peak coincides with the late stage of the 2000s commodity supercycle. We therefore test whether the historical effect is gated by exposure to mineral rents.

Table~\ref{tab:mineral} splits the DMSP 1992--2013 sample by whether a country falls in the top quartile of average mineral rents (World Bank WDI). On the WDI-merged sample, the baseline coefficient is 0.0125 ($p = 0.035$), essentially identical to the unrestricted 0.0128 reported above. Excluding top-quartile mineral-rent countries, that coefficient falls to 0.0038 ($p = 0.526$). The historical effect is entirely concentrated in the mineral-rent quartile, where the coefficient is 0.0456 ($p = 0.006$). The SSA decomposition tells the same story: excluding mineral-rent countries, the SSA effect drops from 0.0442 ($p = 0.011$) to 0.0090 ($p = 0.659$), while SSA mineral-rent countries alone yield 0.0967 ($p < 0.001$). Outside SSA, neither slice produces a statistically distinguishable effect, so the mineral-rent split does not simply relabel the SSA decomposition: it sharpens it.

Two additional patterns are consistent with a fiscal-slack reading. First, within mineral-rent economies the effect is roughly invariant to V-Dem electoral democracy: strict autocracies (0.0657, $p = 0.098$) and non-autocracies (0.0498, $p = 0.003$) deliver similar point estimates, suggesting that discretionary rent revenue substitutes for the institutional channels that would otherwise constrain it. Second, the effect is gated by leader tenure: it roughly doubles after a leader's fifth year in office (0.0641, $p = 0.004$) compared with the first four years (0.0313, $p = 0.060$), consistent with the lead time required to plan and direct large regional investments. The mineral-rent split is therefore not just a country-composition artifact; it picks out the kind of state in which the canonical effect appears.

We read this as a diagnostic rather than a clean causal estimate. The mineral-rent variable is country-level and slow-moving, so the split absorbs other features of resource-rich states. What it does pin down is that the late-2000s peak and the SSA concentration share a common driver: the commodity-boom-era fiscal position of mineral-rich states.

\subsection*{Standalone VIIRS in later years}

The later null is not just a DMSP-to-VIIRS splice artifact. In a standalone VIIRS-only panel, the pooled 2014--2024 lag-0 coefficient is negative at $-0.0354$ ($p = 0.002$), and the early VIIRS bin 2014--2016 is also negative at $-0.0495$ ($p = 0.001$). Standalone VIIRS therefore does not restore the canonical positive birth-region activity effect in later years.\footnote{In additional geography splits, the negative VIIRS-only pattern is concentrated outside Sub-Saharan Africa: the non-SSA pooled 2012--2024 coefficient is $-0.0473$ ($p < 0.001$), while the SSA coefficient is positive but imprecise at 0.0270 ($p = 0.378$). We do not have a convincing explanation for that later non-SSA sign reversal, so we treat it as an unresolved fact rather than a headline result.}

\section{Subsample Search}

If the pooled post-2005 activity reduced form masks heterogeneity, the most plausible places to look are settings where the original favoritism mechanism should be strongest or easiest to measure. We therefore test four families of subsamples: geography, political capacity, measurement headroom, and pollution-support samples. The geography cut captures Hodler and Raschky's finding of especially strong effects in Sub-Saharan Africa. The political-capacity cut isolates long-tenure leaders and long-tenure autocrats. The headroom splits ask whether DMSP saturation hides effects in already-bright districts. The pollution-support splits ask whether environmental outcomes are only informative in countries with enough NO$_2$ variation.

The role of this section is confirmatory rather than exploratory. No post-2005 cut restores a stable positive activity effect once the break appears. In Table~\ref{tab:subsample-firststage}, the other theory-favored post-2005 samples all fail to revive the reduced form: long-tenure autocrats are consistent with zero and too imprecise to distinguish from the historical effect, dropping high-luminosity districts does not help, and the high-NO$_2$ country sample remains near zero.

The harmonized 2005--2019 panel gives the most suggestive, though still inconclusive, later pattern. Africa (0.0262, $p = 0.167$) and Sub-Saharan Africa (0.0315, $p = 0.135$) are larger than the pooled null, but neither clears our minimum-support rule of a positive coefficient above 0.005 with $p < 0.10$. We therefore treat pollution outcomes in these samples as diagnostic rather than confirmatory (Table~\ref{tab:subsample-pollution}).

\section{Pollution-Side Results}

\subsection*{Pollution outcomes in the 2005--2019 sample}

Pollution estimates test whether the post-2005 attenuation reflects a shift from the quantity to the composition of favored growth. In the pooled 2005--2019 sample (Table~\ref{tab:pollution}), the lag-0 nightlights coefficient is near zero, the lag-0 NO$_2$ coefficient is positive at 0.0308 ($p = 0.045$), and the lag-0 pollution-intensity outcome is also positive at 0.0333 ($p = 0.063$). These point estimates go against a cleaner-growth interpretation. At the same time, Table~\ref{tab:cluster-robustness} shows that the NO$_2$ estimates become less precise under country clustering, so they are better read as suggestive evidence against green favoritism than as a standalone positive pollution finding.

\subsection*{Autocracy and PM$_{2.5}$ extensions}

The heterogeneity checks do not recover the later activity effect either. Restricting the sample to country-years with $v2x\_polyarchy < 0.3$ yields a lag-0 nightlights coefficient of 0.0111 ($p = 0.574$) in the DMSP 1992--2013 sample and 0.0076 ($p = 0.552$) in the DMSP 2005--2013 sample. These estimates are positive but too imprecise to rescue the historical result.

The PM$_{2.5}$ extension tells the same story. In longer windows, PM$_{2.5}$ level regressions are positive rather than negative, and in the overlapping 2005--2019 sample the PM$_{2.5}$ pollution-intensity outcome is also not negative. The lag-2 estimate in Table~\ref{tab:pollution} is positive and statistically significant, though we read it cautiously given multiple testing across lags. On balance, pollution outcomes do not explain the weaker post-2005 nightlights result as a shift toward cleaner birth-region growth.

\section{Discussion}

The main result is a temporal break, not just a generic later-period attenuation. Within DMSP, the birth-region activity effect is modest in early bins, strongest in the late 2000s, and then much weaker by 2012--2013. The break therefore starts before any switch to later satellite products. The historical positive effect is concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa, but short post-2005 windows do not preserve that pattern cleanly, and standalone VIIRS does not restore it in later years.

The mineral-rent decomposition narrows the candidate explanations sharply. The most consistent reading is that the late-2000s peak reflects an unusually rent-rich window for a small set of mineral-dependent SSA economies, and that the post-2011 break tracks the end of the commodity supercycle rather than a measurement transition. Two features of the mineral-rent estimates support that reading: the effect is independent of V-Dem electoral democracy, consistent with discretionary rent revenue bypassing formal accountability, and it doubles after a leader's fifth year in office, consistent with the lead time required to direct large regional investments. Other candidates---increased transparency in the 2010s, weaker political support from shorter observed spells, reduced DMSP headroom---remain compatible with the data but are not needed to fit the temporal pattern, and the high-luminosity-drop subsamples already rule out a pure saturation story.

The pollution analysis narrows one explanation for that break. If later favoritism shifted from more growth to cleaner growth, pollution intensity should fall in leader birth regions even when nightlights do not rise. Instead, the pooled pollution estimates are zero to positive, and the negative Africa and Sub-Saharan Africa intensity point estimates in Table~\ref{tab:subsample-pollution} are too imprecise to carry the cleaner-growth interpretation. The paper therefore does not solve why the nightlights reduced form breaks after the late 2000s, but it does show that the break is broad and is not explained by a simple shift toward greener birth-region favoritism.
