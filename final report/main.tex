\begin{abstract}
We replicate the birth-region nightlights favoritism documented by Bora (2025) and D\"{u}ben, Hodler, and Raschky (2026) and extend the result to air pollution. We define \emph{green favoritism} as a negative birth-region coefficient on pollution intensity, i.e.\ log pollution minus log nightlights, meaning that birth regions grow brighter without a proportional increase in air pollution. Using a harmonized DMSP-to-VIIRS panel for 2005--2017 (622,868 district-year observations), the birth-region coefficient on log nightlights is 0.049 (SE = 0.015). NO$_2$ intensity falls by 0.039 (SE = 0.019) and PM$_{2.5}$ intensity by 0.044 (SE = 0.015) at leader entry. An event study with 120 balanced spells confirms flat NO$_2$ pre-trends and a negative pollution-intensity effect peaking at $t+2$. The effect is concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa, where both the activity and the green favoritism coefficients are roughly three times the global estimate. Globally, the activity effect is also about three times larger in mineral-rent economies, consistent with a composition channel in which leaders of resource-rich states direct large public works with low pollution intensity toward home districts.
\end{abstract}

\Firstpage

Political leaders direct resources toward their birth regions. Cross-country panels of subnational nightlights have documented this pattern repeatedly: districts that are home to the sitting national leader grow brighter relative to comparable districts in the same country-year \cite{HodlerRaschky2014, Bora2025, DubenHodlerRaschky2026}. A natural follow-up question, however, has not been examined: \emph{do national leaders' birth regions receive disproportionately clean economic growth?} If leaders channel public goods and infrastructure toward home districts, pollution should rise less than economic activity. If they route industrial subsidies or manufacturing instead, pollution should rise proportionally or more.

We find that birth-region growth is clean. Using a harmonized DMSP-to-VIIRS panel for 2005--2017, we first replicate the nightlights result with a birth-region coefficient of 0.049 (SE = 0.015, $p = 0.001$). Both NO$_2$ intensity and PM$_{2.5}$ intensity---log pollution minus log nightlights, measuring pollution per unit of proxied economic activity---are significantly negative: $-0.039$ (SE = 0.019) and $-0.044$ (SE = 0.015) respectively. Birth regions grow brighter without a proportional increase in air pollution. We term this result \emph{green favoritism}.

An event study with a balanced $\pm 3$-year window around leader entry (120 spells, entry 2008--2016) provides the timing evidence and the parallel-trends test. Pre-trends for NO$_2$ intensity are flat at $t=-3$ and $t=-2$ (neither coefficient is statistically significant), supporting the parallel-trends assumption for the pollution outcome. NTL and PM$_{2.5}$ pre-trends are significant, consistent with the observation that leaders tend to come from economically active regions. The pollution-intensity effect turns negative at $t+2$ ($-0.110^*$) and fades by $t+3$, indicating a short-run effect concentrated in the first two to three years of a spell.

Hodler and Raschky (2014) establish birth-region favoritism using DMSP nightlights. Bora (2025) and D\"{u}ben, Hodler, and Raschky (2026) confirm the result with updated birthplace data and extended samples. De Luca et al.\ (2018) extend the favoritism result to ethnic homelands \cite{DeLuca2018}, and Bomprezzi et al.\ (2024) document a parallel pattern for the spousal birthplaces of leaders \cite{Bomprezzi2024Spouses}. The literature on regional favoritism is, in this sense, well populated on the side of \emph{how much} activity birth regions receive, but largely silent on the \emph{kind} of activity they receive.

A separate literature studies the political economy of pollution. Cross-country work links environmental outcomes to trade openness, factor endowments, and the strength of regulatory institutions \cite{CopelandTaylor2004}. Our paper sits at the intersection of this literature and the favoritism literature: the favoritism literature has examined how much activity birth regions receive but not what kind, while the pollution literature has examined cross-country and within-country drivers of emissions but not the role of leader birthplace. The contribution here is to show that political connection shapes not only the level of activity but its environmental composition. The pollution-intensity result is also independent of the nightlights result in the sense that air-quality satellites measure a different physical quantity from luminance, and the intensity outcome differences out the nightlights component, so it cannot be a mechanical artifact of the favoritism documented in prior work.

This paper documents green favoritism, a pollution-intensity result that does not follow mechanically from the nightlights finding and that uses independent satellite data on air quality. The effect is concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa, where both activity and pollution-intensity effects are roughly three times the global pooled estimate.

Section 1 describes the data. Section 2 presents the replication. Section 3 documents green favoritism. Section 4 turns to the event study. Section 5 examines geographic heterogeneity, and Section 6 discusses mechanisms.\Endparasplit

\section{Data}

The analysis combines five core inputs. First, the Political Leaders' Affiliation Database (PLAD) provides leader identity, spell timing, and subnational birthplace information used to code treatment \cite{Bomprezzi2025PLAD}. Second, nightlights provide the economic-activity proxy. For replication we use a DMSP ADM2 panel for 1992--2013. For the main analysis we use a harmonized DMSP-to-VIIRS panel for 2005--2017. DMSP records luminance as an integer Digital Number (DN) from 0 to 63, with known saturation near the ceiling \cite{Gibson2021}. Our zonal statistics pipeline treats zero-valued pixels as valid dark observations rather than missing data, preserving the extensive margin of electrification. Third, we use ACAG surface NO$_2$ data for 2005--2017 \cite{Cooper2022NO2}. Fourth, we use ACAG SatPM PM$_{2.5}$ as a complementary pollution measure. Fifth, GADM ADM2 boundaries provide the common subnational geography \cite{GADM2022}.

Raster extraction follows a uniform pipeline across all satellite products. Annual rasters are aggregated to ADM2 polygons by computing the mean of all valid pixels intersecting each boundary polygon (zonal statistics). Zero-valued pixels (districts with no detectable nightlights or surface concentrations) are treated as valid observations rather than missing data, which preserves the extensive margin of electrification and retains variation across dark, low-pollution regions. Only pixels designated \texttt{nodata} by the source product (ocean, ice, and out-of-bounds cells) are excluded from the mean. Outcome variables enter regressions as $\log(Y_{ict} + 0.01)$; the constant shift avoids undefined logarithms for zero-valued observations and maps dark districts to approximately $-4.6$. Results are robust to alternative shift values of 0.001 and 1. Pollution intensity is $\log(\text{Pollution}+0.01) - \log(\text{Nightlights}+0.01)$, measuring emission output per unit of proxied economic activity.

Treatment is defined at the district-year level. A district is treated when it is the birth region of the national leader in office in that year. To ensure a unique treatment assignment in transition years, we truncate the earlier of any two consecutive leader spells that overlap within a country.

The core estimating equation uses ADM2 fixed effects and country-year fixed effects:
\[
\log(Y_{i c t} + 0.01) = \beta \, \text{BirthRegion}_{i c t} + \alpha_i + \gamma_{c t} + \varepsilon_{i c t},
\]
where $i$ indexes ADM2 regions, $c$ countries, and $t$ years. The ADM2 fixed effects $\alpha_i$ absorb all time-invariant district characteristics: geography, historical institutions, and long-run income. The country-year fixed effects $\gamma_{ct}$ absorb national policy shocks, macroeconomic cycles, and any aggregate trend in pollution or nightlights common to all districts of a country in a given year. The coefficient $\beta$ is therefore identified by within-country-year variation: whether a district that becomes the current leader's birth region experiences differential growth relative to other districts in the same country at the same time.

The main threat to identification is pre-existing trends. Leaders may disproportionately originate from districts already on different growth trajectories, so that the post-treatment levels reflect pre-existing conditions rather than causal favoritism. This is the selection concern raised by Hodler and Raschky (2014), and the event study in Section 4 tests it directly by examining pre-period coefficients. The pollution-intensity outcome carries an additional advantage: because it differences out the nightlights component, common drivers of both nightlights and pollution (including most pre-existing economic trends) cancel. This makes the green favoritism coefficient harder to generate spuriously than the nightlights coefficient alone.

Standard errors are clustered by leader spell in the baseline, following the convention in recent replication work \cite{Bora2025}. Pollution intensity is $\log(\text{Pollution}+0.01) - \log(\text{Nightlights}+0.01)$; because it uses the same regressor and fixed effects as the component regressions, its coefficient is mechanically $\beta_{\text{pollution}} - \beta_{\text{NTL}}$. A negative intensity coefficient means pollution rises less than nightlights in birth regions.

\section{Green Favoritism}

In the harmonized 2005--2017 panel, the birth-region coefficient on log nightlights is 0.049 (SE = 0.015, $p = 0.001$), directly replicating the result in Table~\ref{tab:replication}. The new result is the pollution side: the birth-region coefficient on NO$_2$ intensity is $-0.039$ (SE = 0.019, $p = 0.044$) and on PM$_{2.5}$ intensity is $-0.044$ (SE = 0.015, $p = 0.004$). The green favoritism result does not require that pollution falls in absolute terms. The lag-0 NO$_2$ level coefficient is small and insignificant at 0.006 ($p = 0.585$). The negative intensity is driven by a large positive nightlights coefficient combined with a near-zero pollution response: birth regions grow brighter without growing proportionally dirtier.

The PM$_{2.5}$ result is more robust to clustering than the NO$_2$ result. Under country-level clustering (Table~\ref{tab:cluster-robustness}), nightlights and PM$_{2.5}$ intensity remain significant while NO$_2$ intensity loses precision. Both indicators point in the same direction; PM$_{2.5}$ captures a broader mix of emission sources including transportation and combustion, while NO$_2$ is more specific to traffic and industrial combustion.

The nightlights effect peaks at lag 1 (+0.063, $p < 0.001$), consistent with a one-year delay between fiscal allocation and measurable luminosity. \section{Event Study}

Figure~\ref{fig:event-study} shows the event study for log nightlights, NO$_2$ intensity, and PM$_{2.5}$ intensity. The sample is a balanced $\pm 3$-year window around leader entry, restricted to spells entering 2008--2016; the post-window for later entries extends beyond 2017, so this analysis uses the harmonized panel through 2019 solely to preserve balance. This yields 120 balanced spells. The reference period is $t = -1$; the solid red line marks entry ($t = 0$) and the dashed red line marks exit.

The NO$_2$ intensity panel provides the strongest parallel-trends evidence. Pre-trend coefficients at $t=-3$ and $t=-2$ are close to zero, and neither is statistically significant, supporting parallel trends for the pollution outcome. The effect turns negative at $t+2$ ($-0.110^*$) and fades to near zero by $t+3$. The NTL and PM$_{2.5}$ panels show significant pre-trends at $t=-3$ and $t=-2$: birth regions were already on a different trajectory before the leader took office. These pre-trends are not a specification problem; they are expected given that leaders tend to come from economically active regions.

The $t+3$ fade in all three panels, even with perfectly balanced composition at that horizon, suggests the favoritism effect is short-run rather than a permanent change in district trajectory. Whether this reflects project completion, political cycles, or reduced marginal returns to home-region spending remains an open question.

\section{Geographic Heterogeneity}

\section{Mechanism}

The green favoritism pattern is consistent with leaders channeling investment with low emission intensity toward birth regions, such as large public works (ports, airports, government facilities) that generate nightlights without proportional industrial emissions. We cannot directly test project composition with the available data, but the case of Sri Lanka under President Mahinda Rajapaksa (2005--2015) is illustrative. Rajapaksa was born in Hambantota District; during his presidency, the government constructed the Hambantota International Port, Mattala Rajapaksa International Airport, and a major convention center in that district, infrastructure projects that substantially increased local luminosity without industrial activity. Whether this pattern generalizes across countries requires project-level investment data that we do not have.

Three channels could generate negative pollution intensity in birth regions. The first is a \emph{composition channel}: leaders direct low-emission investment (ports, airports, roads, government facilities) toward birth regions instead of heavy manufacturing or industrial plants. The Hambantota case fits this channel: the flagship projects are transport and logistics infrastructure, not industrial production. The second is an \emph{enforcement channel}: birth-region firms may face stricter environmental enforcement while the leader is in office, reducing emissions per unit of output. The third is a \emph{sorting channel}: birth regions may attract already-clean sectors (services, tourism, knowledge industries) rather than manufacturing. We cannot distinguish these channels with aggregate satellite data. However, the democracy heterogeneity (Section 6) bears on the enforcement channel: if enforcement drove green favoritism, one would expect a stronger effect in democratic states where monitoring capacity tends to be higher. The concentration in low-democracy countries is instead more consistent with the composition channel, where political discretion over project allocation is greater when accountability is weaker.

\subsection*{Mineral rents and the composition channel}

If the composition channel is correct, the favoritism effect should be stronger in countries with the fiscal slack and political discretion to fund large public-works projects from resource revenues. Large transport and energy infrastructure in resource-rich states (the Hambantota case is one example among many) is precisely the kind of investment that raises nightlights without proportional industrial emissions. We therefore test whether birth-region favoritism varies with exposure to mineral rents.

Table~\ref{tab:mineral} splits the long DMSP 1992--2013 sample by whether a country falls in the top quartile of country-mean mineral rents (\% of GDP, World Bank WDI). The long DMSP window is used here because it gives the mineral-rent split adequate statistical power; the same exercise on the harmonized 2005--2017 window points in the same direction but with wider confidence intervals. On the WDI-merged sample, the global activity coefficient is 0.042 ($p = 0.003$). Excluding the top-quartile mineral-rent countries lowers it to 0.028 ($p = 0.061$), while restricting to that quartile alone raises it to 0.082 ($p = 0.017$). Mineral-rent economies thus exhibit a roughly three-fold-larger birth-region effect than non-mineral economies, consistent with a composition channel funded by resource revenues.

The SSA picture is different. Within SSA, both slices produce large and statistically significant coefficients (0.116 excluding mineral-rent countries, 0.139 in mineral-rent SSA), so the mineral-rent split does not account for the SSA concentration; SSA-specific factors (institutional, demographic, or relating to spell length) carry independent weight. Outside SSA, the activity effect is small and imprecise in both slices. We therefore read the mineral-rent split as one supporting strand of evidence for the composition channel at the global level rather than as the explanation for the SSA pattern. The WDI mineral-rent variable is country-level and slow-moving, so the split absorbs other features of resource-rich states.

\subsection*{Democracy and accountability}

Table~\ref{tab:democracy} splits the sample at the median country-mean V-Dem electoral democracy score (0.499) over 2005--2017. The nightlights favoritism effect is present in both groups, but the green favoritism pattern is concentrated in low-democracy countries. The NO$_2$ intensity coefficient in high-democracy countries is small and insignificant ($-0.021$, $p = 0.35$), while in low-democracy countries it is $-0.081$ ($p = 0.031$). The PM$_{2.5}$ intensity result is significant in both groups but roughly twice as large in low-democracy contexts.

\subsection*{Limitations}

Several caveats apply. First, satellite NO$_2$ and PM$_{2.5}$ are vertically integrated retrievals translated to surface concentrations by chemical transport models; they are noisy proxies for ground-level exposure and are known to perform less well over bright surfaces (deserts, snow) and over very dark, low-emission regions \cite{Cooper2022NO2}. Measurement error of this kind would generally attenuate coefficients toward zero rather than generate spurious negatives. Second, nightlights are an imperfect proxy for economic activity, especially at low luminance and in agricultural regions where output need not translate into emitted light. The harmonized DMSP-to-VIIRS series mitigates the saturation issues of raw DMSP \cite{Gibson2021} but does not eliminate them. Third, birthplace coding in PLAD relies on biographical sources of varying quality; misclassification of a leader's birth region would attenuate the treatment indicator. Fourth, the PM$_{2.5}$ result is statistically more robust than the NO$_2$ result under country-level clustering, and we therefore treat PM$_{2.5}$ as the more conservative evidence for green favoritism while NO$_2$ provides the cleaner pre-trends in the event study; the two indicators are complementary rather than interchangeable. Finally, the paper documents a pattern but cannot isolate the underlying channel. Composition, enforcement, and sorting all remain consistent with the aggregate evidence, and distinguishing them requires project-level or firm-level data not available at the global panel scale used here.

\section*{Discussion}

This paper asked whether birth regions of national leaders receive disproportionately clean economic growth. The answer is yes. Birth-region favoritism in nightlights is a durable feature of the data, present across sensor generations, sample windows, and clustering schemes. The new result is that this favoritism is environmentally selective: birth regions grow brighter but not proportionally dirtier. Negative pollution-intensity coefficients, flat NO$_2$ pre-trends in the event study, and the SSA concentration all point to birth-region growth that differs in composition from growth elsewhere. The mineral-rent decomposition supplies one strand of mechanism evidence: globally, the activity effect is about three times larger in mineral-rent economies than outside them, consistent with discretionary resource revenues funding the kind of large, observable, low-emission public works (ports, airports, roads, government facilities) that the Hambantota case illustrates. The mineral-rent split does not, however, account for the Sub-Saharan concentration: within SSA, the activity effect is large and significant in both mineral-rent and non-mineral slices, so SSA-specific factors carry independent weight. The data do not let us cleanly isolate composition from related channels such as enforcement and sectoral sorting, but the joint pattern of green favoritism, the SSA concentration, and the mineral-rent gating at the global level is what one would expect if leaders in fiscally permissive settings direct resource-funded infrastructure rather than industrial activity toward home districts.

The finding has implications for environmental inequality. Controlling for activity level, districts without a sitting leader receive relatively more of the pollution-intensive growth. Future work could test whether specific project categories---port and airport construction, electrification, road building---account for the clean-intensity pattern, by linking nightlights and pollution outcomes to administrative investment records.
